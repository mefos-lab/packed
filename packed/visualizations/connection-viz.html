Why this is a connection graph, not a money-flow diagram

Money entering an intermediary committee is commingled. A leadership PAC that receives $5,000 from one donor holds it in a single account, and when it later gives $10,000 to a candidate, no part of that sum is traceable to the original donor. A weighted flow diagram would draw a thick line straight through that hop and invent a figure nobody can defend.

Connectivity survives what amounts do not. That two entities are linked by three separate disclosed routes is a fact, and it needs no dollar figure attached to the far end. So every edge here declares which kind of claim it is, and nothing is ever summed along a path — there is deliberately no total anywhere in this interface.

Routes between two entities

A single connection between two entities is ordinary. Several separate routes is the finding — it means the relationship shows up in more than one filing, in more than one form. Routes are listed and counted, never added together: the same money commonly appears on more than one of them.

Whose committee seats the money lands on

Giving to members of the committee with jurisdiction over your industry is lawful and routine. The signal is concentration, not the existence of a contribution. A dollar is counted once per committee its recipient sits on, so these totals intentionally sum to more than the money given — the question is exposure per committee, not a partition of the dollars.

Colleagues giving together

This is the shape of a reimbursement scheme, in which a company funds contributions made in employees' names. It is equally the shape of a lawful workplace fundraising drive, and nothing in the filings separates the two. Treat every cluster as a lead to check, never a finding. Clusters where every donor gave an identical amount are marked, because a reimbursement tends to be a fixed sum per person while colleagues giving independently rarely match to the dollar — suggestive, not decisive.

Committees a firm's people came from

Moving from government into lobbying is lawful and extremely common. What the disclosure supports is showing where a firm's inside experience is concentrated, not an allegation about any of it. The two routes are kept apart because they are not the same claim: served the committee is disclosed directly, while staffed a member credits someone to whatever seats that member holds today.

Most lobbyists disclose no covered position at all, and references to members who have left office do not resolve against current rosters. Absence of a tie here is not evidence there is none.

What share of spending reaches candidates

A low share is lawful and often entirely legitimate — an independent-expenditure or issue-advocacy group is not supposed to be giving money to candidates, and a young committee spends before it gives. No law sets a required ratio. What this answers is the narrow factual question of where a donor's money went, which a committee's name and its fundraising appeals do not reveal. Percentages are the FEC's own, surfaced rather than recomputed.

Who funds the spenders

Every other view here watches money leave. An outside group running advertisements is spending somebody's money, and the advertisement never says whose. Where one backer supplies most of the funding, the committee is better described as that backer's vehicle than as an independent actor — that is a factual reading of the filings, not an accusation. Organisation, committee or individual comes from the filer's own declaration, not guessed from the name.

Money in cannot be matched to money out. A committee's receipts are commingled, so no contribution here can be traced to any particular expenditure the committee made. Both hops are disclosed; the chain between them is not a sum.

What this cannot show

Every pattern that fed this view carries its own interpretive limits. A merged picture is exactly where those get lost, so they are collected here in full.
